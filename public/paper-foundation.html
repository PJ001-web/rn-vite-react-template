The Foundation: Strategic Resilience for Sustainable Impact

White paper6 min read
← All insights

A framework, written down.

There is no verb for resilience. There is also no agreed definition. That absence is not a minor theoretical gap; it has practical consequences. It means organisations invest in resilience without knowing what outcome they are building towards. It means boards govern against a concept no one in the room has defined in the same way.

Strategic Resilience for Sustainable Impact started there.

The research set out to answer a deceptively simple question: how does resilience become strategic? To answer it, I mapped the concept across its entire landscape through 47 definitions, spanning ecology, engineering, psychology, economics, organisational theory, and public policy. The literature is vast. What it reveals, when read together, is a structural flaw that runs through almost all of it.

Every existing model treats resilience as pointing towards one of three possible ends: you recover to where you were, you adapt to where you need to be, or you transform into something new. Recovery, adaptation, transformation - these are the three outcomes the field has always identified. What the field has never done is hold them together.

The insight that emerged from the research is this: resilience becomes strategic not when an organisation pursues one of these outcomes well, but when it pursues all three simultaneously. Each demands a different strategy. Each pulls on different capabilities. The organisation that can hold the tension of all three at once - absorbing the shock, adjusting to the new conditions, and using the disruption to move ahead - has entered a different operating space. That is the Resilience Nexus. That is where resilience stops being a cost and starts being an economic model.

This was not a theoretical proposition. It was tested across 23 primary interviews with senior practitioners and policymakers at the World Economic Forum, McKinsey, the European Union, the Stockholm Resilience Institute, the OECD, the UN, and governments including Malawi and Estonia. What came back was consistent: the organisations navigating disruption most effectively were not the ones with the best crisis response. They were the ones that had learned to think across all three outcomes at the same time, without collapsing into any one of them.

The Malawi government illustrated it most directly embedding absorptive, adaptive, and transformative agendas into a single coordinated framework, so that every intervention was serving all three ends rather than trading one off against another. Estonia's second independence told a different version of the same story: a nation that refused to frame its position as adversity and used the full force of the disruption to leapfrog ahead while simultaneously rebuilding stability.

What the research also found, and this is the contribution that matters most for practice, is that the capability required to operate inside the Nexus is not structural. You cannot engineer it into a governance framework or a risk register. It is a leadership capability. The research named it navigational leadership: the capacity to steer through ambiguity and convergence without a fixed map. To hold multiple outcomes in parallel. To make decisions before clarity arrives. To pivot without losing the thread of purpose.

This is the dimension most consistently absent in the organisations that struggle. Not information. Not resources. Not even strategy. The ability to navigate the Nexus from the inside, in motion, under pressure.

The Resilience Index is built to surface it. Every score it produces is a reading of where an organisation sits across all three outcomes, and whether its leadership is navigating the convergence or drifting towards one end at the expense of the others.
