Biosecurity is economic infrastructure, not a health issue

White paper6 min read
← All insights

Why the first family of the Resilience Index proves the wider model.

Biosecurity sits underneath the economy. It belongs with the power grid, the payment system and the ports: the infrastructure everything else runs on and no one prices until it fails. A foot-and-mouth outbreak closes export markets within hours. Rift Valley fever moves through livestock and takes rural incomes, trade access and food supply with it. The bill lands on agriculture, then trade, then public finances, then the political system that has to absorb the shock. By the time it registers as a health matter it has already moved through the economy.

Filing biosecurity under health is what keeps it underfunded. Health budgets treat it as one priority competing against many, measured by cases and outcomes. Read as infrastructure, the question changes: what does it protect, what does its failure cost, and what return does resilience earn. Those are questions a finance ministry, a board and an investor can act on, because they are posed in the terms those people already use to allocate capital.

This is why biosecurity is the first family of the Resilience Index rather than an application chosen for topical reasons. The framework holds that resilience becomes strategic when an entity carries three outcomes at once: persevere, adapt, transform. Protect what matters, adjust as conditions change, build towards what comes next.

Biosecurity demands all three in plain view.

A country has to hold the line against an active outbreak, adapt its surveillance and trade posture as pathogens and partners shift, and invest ahead of threats that have not arrived, from vaccine stockpiles to detection systems whose value shows only when they are never needed. Lean on any one outcome and the other two expose the gap. Stockpile without surveillance and the reserve sits in the wrong place. Watch without investing and the warning arrives with nothing behind it.

That is what makes it a proof point rather than a case study.

The wider claim of the Index is that resilience can be measured on one methodology across nations, sectors and institutions, and that the same six dimensions resolve into a score a decision-maker can govern against.

Biosecurity tests the claim under load.

The data is real, the shocks are real, the cost of getting it wrong is measured in closed borders and lost output rather than in theory. If the framework produces a score that holds up here, against pathogens that do not respect the boundaries between health, trade and security, it holds across domains where the pressures are less entangled.

The entanglement is the point. Biosecurity refuses to stay in one column. It runs through animal health, human health, food systems, trade policy, national security and public finance at the same time, which is exactly the multi-dimensional, multi-timescale problem the Resilience Nexus was built to hold. A framework that can score resilience in a domain this convergent is showing what it can do everywhere else. The first family earns the rest.

Priced as infrastructure, biosecurity becomes something a board can weigh, a government can benchmark and an investor can carry into a thesis.

That shift, from health cost to economic asset, is the same shift the Index makes everywhere it goes. Biosecurity is where it becomes hardest to argue with.

Put a number on your resilience.
